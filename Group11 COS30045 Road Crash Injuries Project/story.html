STORYLINE 01 OF 06 · NATIONAL TREND

A decade of crashes, and a growing older share

First the whole picture: every road crash hospitalisation in Australia, year by year. Then the question this site is about: how much of it is people aged 65 and over?

Road crash hospitalisations rose from 34,033 in 2011 to a peak of 39,864 in 2019. They fell to 37,959 in 2020, the first year of COVID-19, when people travelled less, and came back to 39,504 in 2021.

…change in hospitalisations from 2011 to 2021

Hospitalisations of people aged 65 and over rose by 41% (3,874 to 5,476), while people under 65 rose by only 13%. So the older share climbed from 11.4% in 2011 to a peak of 14.4% in 2019, dipped in 2020 and was 13.9% in 2021.

…of all road crash hospitalisations were people aged 65+ in 2021: about 1 in 7, up from 1 in 9

STORYLINE 02 OF 06 · AGE GROUPS

Who is rising?

Age groups are very different in size, so every group starts at 100 in 2011. A line at 150 means 50% more hospitalisations than in 2011.

People aged 65 to 74 grew the fastest, from 1,968 to 3,018 hospitalisations. People aged 75 and over grew from 1,906 to 2,458. Meanwhile the 17 to 25 group, the one most crash campaigns target, stayed almost flat.

…growth for ages 65 to 74, the fastest of any age group

The 40 to 64 group is still the biggest in raw numbers every year. This chart is about speed of growth, not size.

STORYLINE 03 OF 06 · ROAD USERS

How they get hurt, and who

Looking only at people aged 65 and over: which road users are behind the growth, and is it men or women?

Cyclists are the stand-out. Older cyclists in hospital went from 327 to 1,034. Motorcyclists went from 203 to 514. Car occupants are still the largest group (3,138 in 2021) but grew far more slowly, and older pedestrians actually fell.

…as many older cyclists in hospital in 2021 as in 2011

Press ▶ to watch the change build year by year, or drag the slider.

Men pull ahead. In 2011 older men and older women were hospitalised in almost equal numbers. Older men then rose from 1,974 to 3,108 (57%) and older women from 1,900 to 2,368 (25%). About three quarters of the extra older men (860 of 1,134) were cyclists or motorcyclists.

…more older men than older women in hospital in 2021

STORYLINE 04 OF 06 · HOSPITAL STAYS

Longer stays

Counting admissions is only half of it. Older patients take longer to recover, so each crash uses more hospital beds.

In 2021, a patient aged 75 or over stayed an average of 10.4 days, compared with about 4 days for adults aged 17 to 39. Within the same year, people aged 65+ stayed about twice as long as people under 65.

…of all road crash bed days in 2021 were used by people aged 65+, even though they were only … of patients

Careful: bed days jump for every age group between 2014 and 2015, which may be a change in how stays were recorded. Compare age groups within the same year.

STORYLINE 05 OF 06 · STATES

Is it the same in every state?

A national trend can hide big differences between places. The state and territory file lets us check the older share in each one.

Yes: in every state with reliable numbers the older share went up. Tasmania rose the most, from 12.4% to 18.3%, followed by WA (9.8% to 14.2%) and NSW (12.6% to 16.2%). South Australia, Queensland and Victoria rose too.

…states had a bigger older share in 2021 than in 2011

ACT and NT are marked with a star. Many of their older age counts were not published (n.p.) because the numbers are small, so their shares are not reliable.

STORYLINE 06 OF 06 · FIRST NATIONS

Not the same for everyone

The First Nations file compares Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people with non-Indigenous people. Their story looks very different.

Road crash hospitalisations of First Nations people almost doubled, from 1,167 in 2011 to 2,220 in 2021. But very few were older: only about 2% were aged 65+, against 14.6% for non-Indigenous people. Almost a third were aged 17 to 25, and nearly a quarter lived in remote areas.

…of First Nations patients were aged 65+ in 2021

These are counts, not rates. The First Nations population is younger on average, which is part of the reason. "Growing older" is mainly a non-Indigenous trend; for First Nations people the burden falls on young adults.

Reality check

Is it just because there are more older people?

Mostly, yes. Australia's population aged 65 and over grew by about 42% between 2011 and 2021 (ABS), almost exactly as fast as their road crash hospitalisations (41%). Per person, the yearly rate stayed about the same.

…older Australians per 100,000 were hospitalised after a road crash, in 2011 and in 2021

So the "growing older group" is mainly a story about an ageing population, not about each older person becoming less safe. The extra load on hospitals is still real, and longer stays (storyline 04) make it heavier. Population figures: ABS estimated resident population for June 2011 and the 2021 Census count, so rates are approximate.

WHAT IT MEANS

An older road, a heavier load

People aged 65 and over are a growing part of road crash hospitalisations in every large state. They are getting hurt more on bikes and motorbikes, men more than women, and each injury keeps them in hospital longer. For First Nations people, though, the burden is still mostly on the young.

For road safety planners

Safe cycling paths and motorbike refresher programs aimed at older riders could target the fastest growing injuries.

For hospitals

Older patients use about a quarter of crash bed days. Planning for longer stays and rehabilitation matters as the population ages.

Read with care

The rise matches the growth of the older population, so the rate per older person stayed about the same. The data shows where the load is growing, not that older people became less safe.
